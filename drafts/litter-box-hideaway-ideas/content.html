Most people want the litter box out of sight. Most cats want it somewhere quiet, easy to reach and clean, with a clear view of who's coming. The best hideaway spots do both: they get the box out of the living room without turning it into a dead-end closet the cat won't walk into. The trick is to plan around the cat first and the décor second, because a hidden box the cat avoids is worse than a visible one it uses.

By Avery Linden · Updated November 2026 · Kids & Pets

The short version

Choose a quiet, low-traffic spot that the cat can reach easily at any time of day, away from its food and water, and not next to noisy appliances. Give the cat a way to see out and, ideally, two ways out. Animal welfare groups generally recommend one box per cat plus one extra, in different parts of the home, and at least one per floor in a multi-level house. Scoop at least once a day; that does more for odor than any cabinet. If your cat starts going outside the box or strains in it, call your vet.

Step 1: Start with what cats need from a spot

Cats are both predators and prey, and they're vulnerable in the box. That's why the advice from shelters and veterinary behaviorists sounds similar: quiet, low traffic, easy to get to, and away from where the cat eats and drinks. The Humane World for Animals advises placing boxes in quiet, low-traffic but accessible spots away from food and water. A box in a busy hallway, next to a washing machine that bangs into a spin cycle, or behind a door that sometimes closes, can be enough to send a cat looking for another spot.

Step 2: Work out how many boxes you need

The common rule is one litter box per cat, plus one. Shelters such as the Richmond SPCA recommend it even for a single cat, which means two boxes. In a home with several floors, put at least one box on each floor. Spread the boxes out instead of lining them up: two boxes side by side can look like one big box to a cat.

Step 3: Make sure the hideaway has a view and an exit

Enclosures fail most often because they feel like a trap. Before you buy a cabinet or commit to a closet, check three things: the cat can see out while it's in the box; there's room to turn around and step in and out without squeezing; and another pet can't block the only way out. If you have more than one cat, or a dog that likes to wait outside the box, two entrances help.

Step 4: Keep the box itself the way your cat likes it

Hiding the box doesn't change what's inside it. Most cats prefer an uncovered box with a large footprint and unscented, clumping litter about a couple of inches deep, although some prefer covered boxes. If you switch to a covered box or a cabinet, keep the old setup available for a while and watch which one the cat uses. Self-cleaning boxes can startle some cats with their noise and movement.

Step 5: Control odor by scooping, not by hiding

Animal welfare groups agree on daily scooping, and the University of Illinois veterinary teaching hospital suggests removing waste at least once a day and topping up litter. Wash the box regularly with mild soap and water rather than strong-smelling cleaners, and replace the litter on a schedule that suits the litter type and number of cats. A hidden box that isn't scooped smells worse than an open one, because the air can't move. Put a small trash can with a lid and a roll of bags next to the box so scooping takes a minute.

Worked example: a one-bedroom apartment with two cats

Two cats means three boxes. Box 1 goes in a litter cabinet in the living room, with the side panel opening toward the room so the cats can see out. Box 2 goes in the bathroom, with a door stop so it can't close. Box 3 goes in a bedroom corner behind a folding screen, well away from the cats' food bowls in the kitchen. A small lidded bin and scoop sit next to each box, and scooping happens morning and evening.

Step 6: Move a box gradually, and watch for warning signs

If you move a box to a new hideaway, add the new box first and leave the old one in place for a week or two, then remove it once the cat is using the new spot. If your cat starts going outside the box, strains in the box, cries while using it, or goes in and out without producing much, call your veterinarian. The Merck Veterinary Manual notes that straining in the litter box can be a sign of a life-threatening emergency, especially in a male cat.

Litter box hideaway checklist
  • Quiet, low-traffic spot the cat can reach day and night.
  • Away from food and water bowls and noisy appliances.
  • One box per cat, plus one; at least one per floor.
  • Boxes spread out, not side by side.
  • The cat can see out, turn around and leave easily; two exits if possible.
  • Doors propped open so nothing can close the cat out.
  • Scooped at least once a day, with a lidded bin nearby.
  • New spot added before the old one is removed.
Can I put the litter box in a closet?

Yes, if the door is removed or held open, the closet is roomy enough that the cat can see out and turn around, and the box is scooped daily. Deep, narrow closets that feel like dead ends are often avoided.

How many litter boxes do I need for one cat?

Many shelters recommend one box per cat plus one, so two boxes for one cat, placed in different parts of the home.

Why won't my cat use the new litter cabinet?

It may feel too enclosed, too small or too unfamiliar. Leave the old box available, make the cabinet entrance bigger or face it toward the room, and try the same litter as before. If your cat goes outside the box, check with your vet to rule out a medical cause.

Is the garage a good place for a litter box?

Usually only as an extra box. Garages can be cold, noisy and closed off, and they often store chemicals. Your cat should always have a box inside the main living area.
